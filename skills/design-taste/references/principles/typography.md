# Design Taste: Typography

> Layer tags: `L1` foundation (always), `L2` tonal preset (only for that tone), `L3` brand-negotiable. `Rnnn` = the comparison round that produced the rule (see ../evidence.md). Non-negotiables in SKILL.md survive any brand override.

- **In type-only work, type becomes the image.** A headline with no picture to support it must be treated as a graphic element, considered for its shape, mass and character, not just its words. `L1` · R010
- **Type as information by default. Type as image only when it's truly the best solve.** Type can serve as both. The default is legible, hierarchical type read as words, even at extreme scale. Turning type into the picture, by cropping it into texture or building words into structures, is reserved for when it is genuinely the strongest answer for the concept, not a stylistic flourish. `L1` · R010, R086
- **A plain neutral face undersells a type-only headline.** When the headline carries the piece, reach for a face with more character, a display face that's genuinely interesting. Neutral faces stay appropriate for supporting text. `L1` · R010, R011
- **A role-based type system: display serif, text serif, neutral sans.** Each face has one job.
  - **Display serif:** the headline only.
  - **Text serif:** the one piece of practical information promoted to be found fast, such as the dates. It echoes the headline's serif voice but is drawn for legibility at smaller sizes.
  - **Neutral sans:** everything else, including the subhead, body, labels and minor details.

  The serif echo ties the top and bottom of the hierarchy together. A serif subhead, an all-serif system, and a mono voice are not preferred. `L1` · R022, R023
- **Let the headline carry the weight, and keep supporting text at one regular weight.** Hierarchy below the headline comes from size, tone and position, not from bold. A bolded subhead or label reads as heavy-handed and interrupts the graceful step down from the headline. Weight contrast belongs to the headline-versus-everything relationship, not to steps among the supporting text. `L1` · R024
- **Emphasize with weight, not italics.** Italics are used sparingly, if at all. To stress a word or line, vary the weight within the same family. Serif italics at display sizes and tight leading are especially risky: their slanted ascenders and descenders collide with neighboring lines. A headline that needs internal emphasis therefore needs a family with a real weight range. `L1` · R025
- **Weight follows meaning.** Within a headline, give weight to the phrase that carries the core idea, usually the subject noun phrase, and set the framing or modifier words lighter. Where possible, let the weight echo what the words say: "The Quiet" set in a light weight literally speaks quietly. `L1` · R027
- **Headline weight jumps stay moderate.** Pair neighboring weights a couple of steps apart, such as light with medium. Extreme pairings, like hairline-thin against heavy bold, are too contrasty and become the subject themselves. `L1` · R024, R027
- **Headline leading: as tight as it can go without a collision, and no looser than it takes to read as one thought.** Close the headline lines up until they read as a single, complete statement. Spacing that's too open breaks a headline into separate, unrelated lines. Stop before any glyph from one line touches a glyph on the next. Set it optically against the actual letter pairs, not by formula: check descenders such as the tail of *Q*, *g* and *y* against ascenders, dots and cap tops in the line below. `L1` · R029
- **Body leading stays moderate.** Comfortable and even, neither cramped nor floating. `L1` · R029
- **Moderate size steps below the headline.** Once the headline has made its dramatic jump, the tiers beneath it step down in moderate, even increments. The subhead must be large enough to be noticed at once, but not so large that it starts competing with the headline. Compressed steps lose the subhead. Dramatic steps over-promote it. Moderate steps read as elegant. `L1` · R031
- **Flush left, ragged right is the default alignment for text.** Never fully justify text, and avoid it in essentially all work. Centering may organize a layout around an axis (R002, R006), but text blocks themselves read flush left. `L1` · R030
- **Case is assigned by tier, and applied to the whole tier.** All-caps belongs to the smallest metadata tier: labels, locations, entry notes and similar supplemental details. At that size, tracked capitals read cleanly and signal "this is metadata." Every element in a tier gets the same case treatment. Capitalizing some metadata items but not others reads as awkward and accidental. Headlines, subheads, body copy and any promoted detail stay in sentence case. `L1` · R028, R029
  - **The promoted date stays in sentence case.** It's larger than the metadata tier and set in the text serif, so it belongs to a different tier. Caps are only for the smallest text. (Correction to R028, given during R029.)
- **Display faces are for display sizes only.** A display face, especially a narrow or high-contrast one, gets harder to read as it shrinks. When an element below headline scale needs the same voice, use a text-optimized companion in the same genre rather than the display face itself. `L1` · R023
- **Character comes from contrast and proportion, not from quirky letterforms.** A good display face earns its personality through stroke contrast, proportion and rhythm. Idiosyncratic individual glyphs, such as oddly shaped *h*, *f* or other letters, slow reading and distract from the word. Choose faces whose letterforms stay classical and legible even at display sizes. `L1` · R011
- **Serif vs. sans follows tone.** Quiet, refined, premium subjects use an editorial serif. Playful, family, approachable subjects use a friendly rounded sans for the display and promoted elements. The neutral sans stays the reading face in both. `L2` · R011, R012, R056
- **For quiet, refined subjects, use a warm editorial serif with a real weight range.** It should be easy to read and have genuine character. In heavier weights it should feel sturdy, even slightly boxy. It must offer enough weights to emphasize by weight. Character beats narrowness: a slightly wider face with warmth outranks a narrow one with a single weight. A neutral grotesque is an acceptable but less expressive fallback. *(Reference example: Newsreader at display optical size. Instrument Serif's look was right, but its single weight ruled it out.)* `L2 · quiet/premium` · R011, R012, R026
- **Character without coldness.** A serif that is too uniform and engineered reads as robotic. A serif that is too idiosyncratic reads as quirky. The target is between the two: humane, editorial warmth in faces that stay classical and legible. `L1` · R011, R026
- **Readability outranks elegance when choosing display faces.** Extreme thick-thin contrast with hairline strokes, as in Didone faces, becomes hard to read even at display size and is rejected. Calligraphic old-style faces can look loosely spaced and awkward at large sizes. The winning face is the one that is easiest to read while still having character. `L1` · R012
- **Judge a typeface by the glyphs in the actual copy.** A face can be right overall and still have one weak character, such as an unattractive capital *Q*. Check the specific letters the headline uses, and if a prominent glyph is unappealing, rewrite around it or choose a sibling face. `L1` · R012
- **Display type should read as cohesive word shapes.** Track display sizes so the letters hold together evenly. Spacing that feels loose reads as awkward, and letters that collide read as crude. `L1` · R011, R012
- **Small caps always get tracking: moderate to generous, never none.** The all-caps metadata tier needs opened letter-spacing to read cleanly at small sizes, from a moderate amount up to somewhat more. Spacing so wide that the letters stop forming words makes reading difficult. Tracking tightens as size grows: display type sits close, and small caps open up. `L1` · R032
- **Measure is a readability constraint, not a layout convenience.** Keep body copy columns narrow enough to read comfortably. Never stretch a text column just because there is room. A body column running the full width of the layout is rejected. Hard ceiling: roughly 55–75 characters per line, and fewer for some faces or content. `L1` · R003, R004, R073
- **Headlines and body copy follow separate width rules.** A headline can span the full content width to break into fewer, more confident lines. Body copy stays at a comfortable reading measure inside that width. `L1` · R004
- **Break headlines by phrase, not by available width.** Line breaks should fall at natural phrase boundaries and produce lines that feel balanced. A break that strands or overloads a line is awkward even when it fits. `L1` · R004
- **Line count follows scale.** A small headline stacked into many short lines feels heavy. The same phrase-broken stack at a larger, display-level size feels earned and fills the space with authority. Choose the size and the break together: more lines are fine when the headline is big enough to carry them. `L1` · R003, R019
